# Review of "NarrativeWiki: Evidence-Grounded Character Wikis at Reader-Selected Narrative Cutoffs"

**Date:** 2026-10-08. **Target:** ACL Rolling Review, October 2026 cycle, which NAACL 2027 (June 1-5) and
COLING 2027 (May 9-14, Macau) share. **Submission deadline: 2026-10-12, anywhere on earth.**
**Reviewed text:** the paper as corrected on this date (46 pages; `docs/paper/NarrativeWiki-paper.pdf`).

## 0. How this review was made, and its limits

- I read all 46 pages, recomputed every per-cutoff cell of both texts from the label files (241 cells, all
  equal), recomputed the hand-typed derived values, fetched the ACL Anthology or arXiv record of every
  reference, read three cited papers where the paper makes a specific claim about them, and read the NAACL,
  COLING and ARR calls.
- The five reports of section 6 are five role-separated passes by one model in one context. They are not
  independent reviewers: their errors are correlated, and the model is of the same family as the paper's first
  annotator (Claude Opus 5.5). Treat agreement between the reports as one opinion stated five ways.
- I corrected the manuscript before reviewing it, at the maintainer's request, so the review is of the
  corrected text and I am not neutral about the corrections (section 2 lists them).
- Not verified: values typed from ledger rows that have no label file behind them (the seeded-search counts,
  the verifier challenge sets, the lexical-screen hits, costs, mention counts in the casebook), the
  screenshots, the correctness of the pipeline code, and which GPT-6 model the Codex agent ran. The prompt
  appendix was scanned for identity strings and placeholders, not read line by line.

## 1. Verdict

**The paper is honest, careful and reproducible, and it is not yet a safe accept.** What it can defend is a
task definition, an evaluation protocol for a whole delivered artifact, and a set of mostly negative findings
about its own system. What it cannot yet defend is the instrument: every label is an LLM's, the LLM that
produced most of them helped build the system, and no human labelled anything independently.

Scores I expect from three typical ARR reviewers (form of 2026):

| Field | Expected | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Soundness | 2.5 to 3 | The claims are hedged to what the evidence supports; the evidence rests on unvalidated LLM labels |
| Excitement | 2.5 to 3.5 | A new problem framing and unusually candid results; one book, small counts |
| Overall | 2.5 to 3 | "Borderline Findings" to "Findings"; a strict reviewer gives 2 (resubmit next cycle) |
| Reproducibility | 4 | Prompts, code, labels, costs and cached outputs; a proprietary generator |
| Datasets / Software | 2 to 3 / 3 | 111 gold facts on one series; a working pipeline and evaluation tools |

My estimate of the outcome, as it stands: main conference at NAACL under 10%; Findings 20 to 30%; COLING, whose
scope has room for narrative and evaluation work, 25 to 35%. With independent human labels on a sample and an
agreement figure (section 8, R1), I would raise each by 10 to 15 points. These are judgements, not measurements.

**Venue.** Both conferences read the same ARR reviews; the primary venue is chosen at commitment (NAACL:
2026-12-23), after the reviews are in. Decide then. COLING is the better fit on scope; the NAACL 2027 theme
track (agentic communication) does not apply.

## 6. Reports

### 6.1 Journal fit

**Fit.** In scope for an ACL venue: generation from long documents, retrieval, factuality and attribution,
contamination. The right ARR area is Resources and Evaluation. The title and abstract pitch a system; the
evidence supports a measurement paper. That mismatch is the main presentation problem (section 7).

**Originality.** The framing is new at these venues: a reference bounded by a reader's position, evaluated as
a whole delivered artifact (titles, links, navigation), with three named channels by which later information
arrives. Prior work bounds what a character knows (TimeChara), what a dialogue agent says (Living the Novel),
or picks among human-written revisions (Jones et al.). No prior work evaluates a generated, cutoff-bounded
reference.

**Significance.** Moderate. The use is real (a shipped product makes the same promise), and two findings
travel beyond novels: an instruction does not enforce a knowledge boundary, and an index built with lookahead
changes which entities exist even when every rendered fact is filtered. The paper does not draw the second
connection (temporal question answering, forecasting evaluation).

**Presentation.** Precise and consistent prose; a dense main text that leans on 35 pages of appendix; internal
metric codes (M1, M3, M4, M8, M12, M13) and system codes (B1-B5, X1, X2) that a reader has to hold.

**Signal.** Findings-level as it stands.

checks do not use the search; per-cutoff cells are all printed; both builds of the system are reported; two
audits that moved results against the authors are described; nothing is combined into one score.

### 6.3 Reviewer 2: domain

**Prior work.** The compact section is now adequate on its own claims. Still absent, and each a question
someone may ask: temporal knowledge graphs and time-aware retrieval (the paper says temporal filtering is not
its contribution; one sentence of positioning would close it); attribution evaluation beyond ALCE (citation
recall and precision in generative search); nugget-based recall with a strict support rule, which the recall
rule of Appendix B follows and does not cite; and the narratological distinction between story time and
discourse time, which is the paper's "reading order, not story chronology".

**The pipeline.** Conventional and sensible: lexical mining, model typing and merging, windowed extraction
with verbatim-quote checks, a verifier, intervals. Three points:

- No ablation. The verifier withholds 21.2% of facts; what that buys in precision, or costs in recall, is not
  measured. Nor is the contribution of each of the three extraction passes.
- The one cutoff-aware idea in entity resolution (never merge a bare name into a fuller name first seen later)
  is evaluated by a screen and by examples.
- **The obvious alternative is not discussed.** The LightRAG baseline is built incrementally ("the index at
  cutoff t is the index at t-1 plus volume t, so no index sees a later volume"). Extraction in NarrativeWiki is
  already per volume; only entity discovery reads the whole series, and it is 1.57 of the 31.80 dollars.
  Section 6.3 says that avoiding construction dependence "costs a build per cutoff" (11.58 and 17.23 dollars).
  A reviewer will ask why entity discovery is not simply run per prefix.

**The regime.** Five volumes fit in one context, so long-context prompting is available and wins on coverage.
The paper says its comparison "may change on series too long for one context". That regime, where a graph
might earn its cost, is the one not tested.

**The treated-text failure is a defect, not a finding.** A prediction was stored as a presumed marriage and
"the page renderer printed a presumed spouse as a spouse". That is a rendering fault of a schema that already
separates presumed from asserted. Fixing it and rebuilding one cell would be a stronger paper than reporting it.

### 6.4 Reviewer 3: perspective

- **The reader is absent.** The paper's own sentence is "whose value to a reader we did not measure". The
  system's distinctive output (relationship pages, a timeline, nearly four times the distinct assertions) is
  judged only for error. Without any reader evidence the comparison is decided on the baselines' ground.
- **A volume is a coarse cutoff.** Readers stop inside books; the product that makes the same promise works at
  the reading position. Chapter cutoffs would multiply the cells and the test of construction dependence.
- **Not all later facts are equal.** A marriage and a school prize both count one. The stated, implied and
  uncertain grades point at a severity scale the paper stops short of.
- **The corpus is the easy genre for the hard problem.** Episodic realist fiction has few concealed identities.
  The failure the paper finds most interesting (identity that depends on later text) is the one this corpus
  exercises least; the genres used in development exercise it most and cannot be reported.
- The paragraph on character portraits in Limitations is about a feature the paper never proposes. Cut it.

### 6.5 Devil's advocate

**The strongest counter-argument.** The paper's safety result is close to a tautology, and its system is not
needed for it. If later text is not in the input, a model cannot copy it; three plain baselines show this as
well as NarrativeWiki does and with fewer disclosures (0 of 220 each against 1). The only non-trivial channel is
the generator's memory, and on that the evidence is four events from a closed-book run and eight from an
instructed one, found by a search the authors show to miss three implied disclosures in ten, judged by a
language model that helped build the system. On the treated text the proposed system is the only
input-bounded one that states a later fact. On every measured quality it loses to retrieval over the prefix,
at eight times the cost, and the authors recommend the baseline. The threat that is specific to this design,
that building from the whole series changes which entities exist, is the one the main metric cannot see and
the authors measure with a mention-count screen. What remains is a careful description of how one team
evaluated its own system on one book with its own annotator. That is a workshop paper or a technical report.

**Alternatives the paper does not consider.** Incremental or per-prefix entity discovery (6.3). Reporting the
prefix build as the system and the full build as the diagnostic. Weighting later facts by severity.

**Perspectives missing.** Readers; fan-wiki editors, who already manage spoilers by policy; rights holders.

**Not defects.** The candour is rare and is the paper's best asset: it reports its first build, the audits
that went against it, the rule that moves its own precision by 0.3, and a recommendation against itself.

## 7. Synthesis

**Agreed by every seat.** (1) The labels need an independent human anchor. (2) One work is not enough to
generalise from, and the paper does not claim to. (3) The contribution is the task, the protocol and the
findings, not the system; the title and abstract should say so. (4) Too much of the argument is in appendices.

**Where the seats differ.** Whether a negative result with a new protocol is enough for Findings. The fit and
perspective seats say yes, citing the reviewer guide on negative results. The methodology seat and the devil's
advocate say only once the instrument is validated, because a protocol paper is a paper about its instrument.
I side with the second view: R1 is cheap, and without it the first weakness in every review will be the same.

**Decision.** Major revision, in journal terms. In ARR terms: an overall score of 2.5 to 3 in this cycle. The
revision that changes the outcome fits before the deadline (R1 to R3); the rest is for the next cycle.

## 9. Questions to be ready for

1. *Why should we trust labels made by the model that helped build the system?* (R1.)
2. *Your system loses on recall, precision, citations and cost. What is the contribution?* The task, the
   protocol, and four findings: instruction does not hold a cutoff; a bounded input keeps later text out and
   not later inferences; construction from the whole series changes which entities exist; closed-book recall
   of a famous work is 0.683 and of the treated one 0.003.
3. *Why not build the graph incrementally?*
4. *Is 1 of 220 against 8 of 220 a difference?* Under independence p is about 0.04; the units are not
   independent; the treated text (0 of 660 for three baselines, 23 of 220 under instruction) is the stronger
   evidence.
5. *Why one book?* Release requires the public domain; the public domain is in the training data; the treated
   text is the answer the paper gives, with its limits.
6. *What is "OpenAI Codex (GPT-6)"?* Name the model.
7. *Is LightRAG's 0.304 at t=1 a fair baseline?*
